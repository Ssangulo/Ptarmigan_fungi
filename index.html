This document compiles methodological details, supplementary analyses, and reproducibility outputs supporting the main manuscript.

Computationally intensive steps (DADA2 denoising, LULU curation, iNEXT3D diversity estimation, GLLVM and Bayesian model fitting, phylogenetic community-structure null models) were executed on a remote Ubuntu-based computing environment. Section 2: Bioinformatics summary and study design

2.1 Study site and sampling design

Willow ptarmigan (Lagopus lagopus) faecal samples were collected at Lifjellet (Lierne municipality, Trøndelag, Norway) across a seasonal contrast: winter (March, snow-covered, browse-dominated diet) versus summer (June, green forage available) — replicated over three years (2022, 2023, 2024). The design is a Season × Year factorial, with repeated sampling of individually identifiable birds (microsatellite-confirmed where possible). Approximately 60 dung samples passed processing, of which ~31 have paired plant ITS2 diet data used for the H2 diet-richness mechanism (Section 7).

Each biological dung sample was amplified in two independent PCR replicates. DNA extraction, ITS2 amplification, demultiplexing (sabre), primer trimming (cutadapt), denoising (DADA2, non-pooled inference), and post-clustering curation (LULU) followed the workflow in Scripts/1_demultiplexing.R–Scripts/3_taxonomic_assignment.R; taxonomy was assigned against the UNITE reference (naïve Bayes, minBoot = 80). Negative, no-template, and extraction-blank controls were sequenced alongside samples and used for contaminant removal in Scripts/4_data_prep.R.

3.2 Coprophilous (dung-specialist) genera (E2)

This subsection addresses exploratory aim E2: documenting the occurrence and relative abundance of Sporormiella-type and other Sordariales taxa used in palynological herbivore-presence reconstructions, to provide a modern avian reference point for a proxy currently calibrated almost entirely on mammalian dung.

The canonical tax table was screened against a list of coprophilous genera comprising the standard palynological set — Sordaria, Podospora, Cercophora, Chaetomium, Schizothecium, Preussia, Sporormiella, Delitschia, Pilobolus, Ascobolus, Coprinopsis, Saccobolus, Sporormia — extended with four further dung-associated genera recovered here: Thelebolus, Coniochaeta, Psathyrella and Stropharia. This is the list used throughout this subsection, for both Table S3 and the read-share test below.

Six of the seventeen genera were detected, across 17 OTUs: Sporormiella (7 OTUs), Thelebolus (4), Psathyrella (2), Stropharia (2), and single OTUs of Coniochaeta and Coprinopsis. The eleven remaining genera — including Sordaria, Podospora and Cercophora, the taxa most often counted in palynological work — do not appear in this dataset at all. 3.4 Dark taxa and UNITE Species Hypothesis characterisation (E1)

Nearly half of the retained fungal OTUs are “dark taxa”: 493 of 1129 (43.7%), accounting for 35.4% of reads that the naïve-Bayes classifier leaves without a confident genus. These are not spread evenly across the design: in the dominance analysis (Section 5.4), winter samples are markedly dark-taxa dominated, with dark taxa making up on average 51.3% of winter reads (a far smaller share in summer), and winter’s single most persistent OTU (OTU1369) is itself a dark taxon. To make this unresolved winter community interpretable, every OTU’s ITS2 sequence was matched against the UNITE SH reference set, which places each sequence into an existing species hypothesis or forms a new (candidate) one, independent of whether a Linnaean name is available.

Most dark taxa are close to known references, and a live-database cross-check further shrinks the genuinely-novel fraction. Against the local reference, of the 493 dark OTUs 143 were placeable to an existing named UNITE SH, 247 formed new (candidate) species hypotheses, and 90 had no SH match; 13 were dropped by ITSx. The novel and no-match sequences were then re-run through the PlutoF web service, whose reference database is larger and more current: this reclassified 79 formerly-novel OTUs across the community — 19 of them dark — as matches to existing named SHs (21 of the 79 resolving to species, and 11 of the 19 dark ones gaining a genus). Table S-E1a reflects this combined result: 162 placeable, 228 candidate-novel, 90 no-match. Each placeable OTU resolves to a stable SH page (https://unite.ut.ee/sh/<code>) carrying the taxon name and its DOI. The best-hit similarity distribution (Figure S-E1a) shows the dark fraction is bimodal — a spike near 100% (OTUs essentially identical to a reference but withheld a genus by the conservative minBoot = 80 threshold) plus a real tail of divergent, candidate-novel lineages.

Section 4: Community composition — H1

H1 predicts seasonal compositional turnover of the dung mycobiome that is reproducible across years. Ordination and tests use robust Aitchison distance on the canonical depth-filtered object (alldat$nopool, 52 samples). H1 interpretation — the two clauses separate. Season is a significant predictor of community composition (PERMANOVA R² = 0.11, F = 6.92, p = 0.002), so the first clause of H1 (seasonal turnover exists) is supported. The second clause (magnitude consistent across years) is not: the Season × Year interaction is significant and substantial (R² = 0.10, F = 3.20, p = 0.011), meaning the size of the seasonal contrast genuinely differs between years.

Table S5b localises that interaction. The seasonal shift is strong in 2023 (R² = 0.24, p = 0.005; between-season centroid distance 35.7), much weaker in 2024 (R² = 0.14, p = 0.127; distance 10.3) and effectively absent in 2022 (R² = 0.02, p = 0.789; distance 6.9) — though this last figure is the one part of the breakdown that is sensitive to the distance measure, see caveat (iii) below. The direction of the shift is the same in all three years — it is the magnitude that varies, dominated by an unusually well-separated 2023. This is exactly the pattern the functional-guild analysis finds independently (Section 8.3: dung-saprotroph share summer versus winter is significant in 2023, marginal in 2024 and not significant in 2022).

The interaction is a difference in location, not merely in spread: the distance-to-Season-centroid model (Table S7) finds a strong Season effect on within-season spread (p < 0.001) but no Season × Year interaction in that spread (p = 0.79). In other words, summer communities are consistently more heterogeneous than winter ones in every year, while how far apart the two seasons sit changes from year to year.

Caveats. (i) Beta-dispersion is significantly higher for summer than winter samples (F = 15.3, p = 0.002; Table S6), so part of the PERMANOVA R² reflects this difference in within-group spread rather than a pure centroid shift (Anderson 2001); this previews Section 5’s finding that summer communities are individually more dominance-skewed. (ii) With three years and 15–19 samples per year, a single atypical year moves the interaction substantially; the per-year tests in Table S5b are correspondingly low-powered (no year has more than 10 samples per season). The safe reading is “seasonal turnover is real and directionally consistent, but its magnitude is not established as constant across years” rather than a positive claim about year-specific ecology.

The two blocks are close to mutually exclusive. The ten winter-enriched OTUs account for 40.6% of the reads of an average winter sample but 3.1% of an average summer one; the ten summer-enriched OTUs account for 54.7% of summer but 6.1% of winter. Their cross-season detections are correspondingly sparse — a median of 3.5 of 26 summer samples for the winter-enriched set, and 1 of 26 winter samples for the summer-enriched set — and six of the twenty are never recovered in the opposite season at all. Across the whole object the same pattern holds at the level of bare presence: of 1,129 OTUs, only 171 (15%) are detected in both seasons, against 265 winter-only and 693 summer-only.

This is corroborated by the distance-based tests, which do not depend on the ranking above. Recomputing the identical Season * Year PERMANOVA on presence/absence alone — discarding every abundance — leaves the seasonal effect essentially intact (Jaccard R² = 0.074, p = 0.001; Sørensen R² = 0.113, p = 0.001) against R² = 0.086 for the full-rank abundance-based Aitchison distance. Season explains as much of who is there as of how much of each there is.

Two qualifications. The blocks are defined by relative read share, so a taxon that is genuinely present but rare in the other season can still appear here as near-absent; the claim these numbers support is that the dominant membership of the two seasons is largely disjoint, not that the seasons share no taxa. And the winter-only/summer-only counts above are inflated by rare OTUs seen in a single sample: restricting to OTUs detected in at least five samples of a season leaves 9 winter-only, 51 summer-only and 11 shared, which preserves the asymmetry but on much smaller numbers.

5.3 Why the two metrics disagree: dominance and evenness

The crossover is not an artifact. iNEXT3D pools a Season’s samples and rewards the total species list (summer’s samples collectively contain more species, so summer wins at every q). Per-sample q1/q2 instead downweight rare taxa and reward within-sample evenness — and individual summer samples are far more dominance-skewed than winter ones, so a single summer sample’s effective diversity is often lower even though its raw species list is longer.

Summer gains many rare taxa (the long tail iNEXT3D detects) on top of one or two OTUs that dominate the read count — richness rises, evenness collapses, and q1/q2 track evenness more than richness.

# Fallback ladder if the full random-effects structure is singular: drop
# (1|indivID_glm) first (many NA-individual singleton placeholders), then

fit_h2_lmm <- function(dat, qcol) {
  ladder <- list(
    "Season + (1|Year) + (1|indivID_glm) + (1|pcr_replicate_id)",
    "Season + (1|Year) + (1|pcr_replicate_id)",
    "Season + (1|pcr_replicate_id)")
  fit <- NULL; re_structure <- NA_character_
  for (rhs in ladder) {
    fit <- tryCatch(lmerTest::lmer(as.formula(paste(qcol, "~", rhs)), data = dat),
                    error = function(e) NULL)
    re_structure <- rhs
    if (!is.null(fit) && !lme4::isSingular(fit)) break
  }
  if (is.null(fit) || lme4::isSingular(fit)) {
    fit <- lm(as.formula(paste(qcol, "~ Season")), data = dat)
    re_structure <- "Season (lm, no random effects)"
  }
  co  <- summary(fit)$coefficients
  est <- co["Seasonsummer", "Estimate"]; se <- co["Seasonsummer", "Std. Error"]
  pv  <- if ("Pr(>|t|)" %in% colnames(co)) co["Seasonsummer", "Pr(>|t|)"]
         else co["Seasonsummer", "Pr(>|z|)"]
  data.frame(metric = qcol, re_structure = re_structure,
             summer_minus_winter = round(est, 3),
             lwr = round(est - 1.96 * se, 3), upr = round(est + 1.96 * se, 3),
             p = round(pv, 4))
}

Section 6: Lineage-specific seasonal responses — GLLVM (H1, OTU-resolved)

Sections 4 and 5 establish that the community shifts between seasons (H1) and that summer samples gain a long rare tail while becoming more dominance-skewed (H2). This section resolves H1 to the level of individual OTUs. We fit a generalized linear latent-variable model (GLLVM, negative-binomial) of per-OTU counts as a function of Season (winter as reference), with a library-size offset and random intercepts for individual bird and PCR replicate; the model is fitted on the full-complexity, one-row-per-PCR-replicate object (Scripts/7_gllvm.R, objects models/fit_nb_2.rds and models/fit_nb_2_2.rds). It answers which lineages drive the turnover — each with its own model uncertainty — and, through its latent variables, describes the residual co-occurrence that remains after Season is accounted for.

Section 7: Diet-tracking and Bayesian models — H2 and H3

7.1 Diet richness and fungal diversity — Bayesian mechanism (H2)

Section 5 established the coarse prediction of H2: summer communities are richer than winter ones, in step with the richer summer diet. Here we test the finer mechanism directly, on the subset of samples for which we have paired plant ITS2 diet data (n = 27 dung samples): does an individual sample’s fungal diversity track the richness of its own dietary plant community? Fungal Hill diversity is modelled as a function of standardised plant (diet) richness, adjusting for Season, in a Bayesian linear model (brms). H2’s mechanism is supported if the plant-richness slope is positive with posterior probability P(slope > 0) > 0.95.

The preregistration specified Year and individual bird as random intercepts. At this matched sample size that structure is unidentifiable — 27 of the 27 samples are distinct individuals and Year spans only three levels — and the random-effects model does not converge. We therefore report two complementary, cleanly-converged fixed-effect models that bracket the estimate: a Season-adjusted model (plant_richness + Season + Year, Year as a fixed effect) and a more parsimonious drop-Year model (plant_richness + Season). Both use weakly-informative standardised priors (slope ~ Normal(0, 1)).

7.2 OTU–plant covariation (H3) - H3 not supported - still working

Section 7.1 asked whether overall fungal diversity tracks overall diet richness. H3 is finer-grained and taxon-resolved: does each fungal OTU covary with particular diet-plant taxa, and — the preregistered contrast — do recognised coprophilous taxa show the diffuse, non-specific plant associations expected of dung specialists dispersed independently of any host plant, while other taxa may show the tight, single-plant covariation that would flag a likely ingested endophyte passenger? Inference is strictly forward-directional: a tight single-plant association is read as evidence of a likely ingested passenger; a diffuse or absent association is not read as evidence of gut/dung residency.

Model. We fit a single hierarchical joint negative-binomial model (brms) to the diet-matched subset. To retain as many matched samples as possible we use the PCR-replicate-level, minimally depth-filtered object (mirroring the GLLVM of Section 6) rather than the collapsed, 10,000-read-filtered object of Sections 4–5; this recovers three additional low-depth winter dung samples that carry paired plant data, raising the matched subset to n = 30 biological samples (60 PCR-replicate libraries; 19 winter, 11 summer). Each fungal OTU count is modelled with a per-replicate log-library-size offset (so shallow libraries are accommodated, not discarded), the six dominant diet-plant genera (rCLR-transformed, standardised) as predictors, Season and Year as population-level covariates, per-OTU varying intercepts and plant slopes (the partial-pooling “joint model”), and a per-OTU biological-sample random effect so the two PCR replicates of a sample are treated as replication rather than independent samples. This substitutes a sample random effect for the preregistered “individual” one (unidentifiable here — nearly every sample is a distinct bird), for the same reason as Section 7.1. Forty-six OTUs present in ≥5 of the 30 samples are modelled. For each OTU we summarise the specificity of its plant coefficients with a Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) of the |plant slopes| — ≈1/6 = 0.17 if covariation is spread evenly across all six genera, up to 1 if concentrated on a single plant — and flag a resolved single-plant association when the 90% credible interval of the OTU’s strongest plant slope excludes zero. The preregistered contrast compares specificity between FUNGuild-flagged coprophilous OTUs and plant-associated (endophyte/phyllosphere/plant-saprotroph) OTUs. The model converged cleanly (maximum R̂ = 1.004, no divergent transitions).

7.3 OTU–plant covariation — GLLVM cross-check (H3)

To check that the H3 verdict of Section 7.2 is not an artefact of one modelling framework, we repeat the analysis with a generalised linear latent-variable model (GLLVM) — the same model-based framework used for the OTU-resolved H1 in Section 6, now with the diet-plant predictors added. Everything about the data is held identical to the Bayesian model: the same PCR-replicate-level matched subset (n = 30 biological samples, 60 libraries; 46 OTUs present in ≥5 samples), the same six standardised diet-plant genera (rCLR-transformed), the same per-library log-size offset, and the same specificity index and preregistered coprophilous-vs-plant-associated contrast. Only the estimator differs, which is the point: agreement across two very different fitting machineries is stronger evidence than either alone.

Model. Each OTU’s counts are modelled with a negative-binomial GLLVM (gllvm), with Season and Year as community covariates, a per-OTU (species-specific) fixed slope for each plant genus, a biological-sample random row effect (so the two PCR replicates of a sample count as replication, not independent samples), and the log-library-size offset. We fit 0, 1 and 2 residual latent variables and select by AIC; here num.lv = 0 is preferred — beyond the sample random effect the latent variables capture no further residual co-occurrence (so, unlike Section 6, there is no residual ordination to show). One difference from the Bayesian model is forced by the software: the natural partial-pooling analog (gllvm’s randomX species-specific random slopes) is silently dropped on this dataset, returning no plant-slope parameters at all. We therefore estimate the plant slopes as fixed per-OTU coefficients with Wald standard errors — an unpooled frequentist estimate that brackets the Bayesian pooled one from the other side. Rare on/off OTUs push their fixed coefficients toward ±∞ (near-separation); as in Section 6 these are flagged (16 of 46 OTUs) and excluded from the specificity ranking, contrast and plots, leaving 30 well-estimated OTUs. Specificity is the same Herfindahl index of the |plant slopes|; a resolved single-plant association here means the 90 % Wald interval of an OTU’s strongest plant slope excludes zero.

Section 8: Functional guilds — FUNGuild

Sections 3–6 characterise the seasonal shift taxonomically — which lineages turn over between winter and summer. This section asks what that community does: each OTU is assigned to a functional guild, and the guild composition of the dung mycobiome is compared across seasons. It is the descriptive counterpart to Section 9, which puts the same trait → season link inside a joint species distribution model. The two are deliberately built on the same classification rule so their results are directly comparable, but they differ in scope: Section 8 works on read shares across all 1129 OTUs of the canonical 52-sample object (no prevalence filter, because this is a description of where the reads go), whereas Section 9 models the 226 OTUs that pass its prevalence threshold. All results here are produced by Scripts/8_functional_guilds.R.

8.1 Guild assignment and the classification rule

Guild annotation uses FUNGuild (Nguyen et al. 2016), run through its command-line tool against each OTU’s own taxonomy string, so that assignments are made at the finest rank available per OTU (species, genus, family or order) rather than by a single-rank lookup. 693 of the 1129 canonical OTUs (61.4%) receive a guild call at a usable confidence ranking; the remaining 436 return no assignment — either because no matching taxonomy record exists in the FUNGuild database or because the match carries no ranked guild.

FUNGuild returns compound guild strings rather than a single label — for example |Dung Saprotroph|-Endophyte-Plant Saprotroph-Undefined Saprotroph. OTUs are collapsed to a single class by a priority rule (first match wins): dung saprotroph → plant-associated → pathotroph, with anything else falling to other assigned or unassigned (no FUNGuild call at all). Coprophily takes precedence deliberately: it is the guild the study is about, and it would otherwise be absorbed into a generic saprotroph category. An OTU counts as a dung saprotroph if FUNGuild lists Dung Saprotroph or its genus is one of fourteen recognised coprophilous genera (the Section 3.2 list, omitting Sporormia, Psathyrella and Stropharia). That difference is immaterial here: Sporormia is absent from the dataset, both Stropharia OTUs are already assigned by their FUNGuild string, and the two Psathyrella OTUs carry 0.04% of reads, so no guild share below changes at the reported precision. This is the identical rule used for the Section 9 guild trait.

Section 9: Joint species distribution model — HMSC (H1 synthesis)

Sections 4–6 treat the seasonal signal one lens at a time: community dissimilarity (H1), per-sample diversity (H2), and per-OTU differential abundance (GLLVM). This section brings them into a single hierarchical model. We fit a joint species distribution model (HMSC — Hierarchical Modelling of Species Communities; Scripts/10_hmsc.R) that simultaneously (i) partitions each OTU’s variance among Season, Year, and the nested sampling structure; (ii) asks whether an OTU’s functional guild predicts how it responds to Season (the trait → environment link); (iii) recovers each OTU’s seasonal niche for a like-for-like cross-check against the Section-6 GLLVM; (iv) estimates the residual co-occurrence left once Season, Year and traits are accounted for; and (v) tests for phylogenetic signal in seasonal niches. It is the model-based synthesis of the H1 story, fitted directly on the corrected canonical data (unlike the Section-6 GLLVM, which predates the depth-filter fix).

9.1 Model, design, and what is (and isn’t) trustworthy

The model is fitted on the one-row-per-PCR-replicate object (alldat_full$nopool; 109 replicate rows from 55 biological dung samples), retaining the 226 OTUs present in ≥ 5 replicates — the same prevalence rule used for the GLLVM/H3 analyses. Two response formulations are fitted in parallel: a CLR-transformed abundance model (Gaussian; CLR is the compositional normalisation in place of a library-size offset) and a presence/absence model (probit). Fixed effects are Season (winter as reference) + Year; the species trait is the five-level functional guild class derived from FUNGuild (dung saprotroph, plant-associated, pathotroph, dark/unassigned, other — assigned by the priority rule in Scripts/10_hmsc.R, counts below). Random effects are latent factors at two levels: biological sample (the dung dropping, grouping its two PCR replicates) and PCR replicate.

The explanatory fit is moderate (community-mean R² ≈ 0.46 for the abundance model) but cross-validated R² is near zero — the model does not predict the fungal community of a dropping it has not seen. Far from undercutting the story, this is the quantitative statement of it: the seasonal and guild effects documented above are real and directional but small against the dominant, idiosyncratic variation among individual droppings. A ptarmigan’s dung mycobiome is shaped by season in a reproducible way, yet remains largely individual — which is exactly what a transient, diet- and propagule-sourced community should look like, and a useful caution for anyone hoping to read season directly off a single sample.

In summary, HMSC does not overturn any earlier result; it unifies them and adds two things the single-lens analyses could not: a variance decomposition that sizes the seasonal signal against individual heterogeneity (and independently recovers the PERMANOVA effect size), and a formal trait- and phylogeny-level account of why the seasonal turnover looks the way it does — a summer-occurring coprophilous dung guild, phylogenetically conserved, riding on top of large among-individual variability.

Figure 2: Winter and summer dung mycobiomes differ in composition, and the size of that difference varies among years. (A) Unconstrained principal coordinates analysis of robust Aitchison distances among the 52 canonical samples, coloured by season and shaped by year. Thin lines join each sample to its season centroid (×). The axis percentages are shares of the rank-3 reconstruction from which vegan computes robust Aitchison distances, not of the full community variation, and the axis titles name that denominator accordingly; under a full-rank Aitchison distance (CLR after a pseudocount) the same two axes take 13.7% and 9.5%, which is the closer approximation to the variance actually explained (Appendix Section 4.1). Winter and summer communities separate along the first ordination axis: Season explains 11% of compositional variance (PERMANOVA p = 0.002; Table S5). The seasonal contrast is not, however, of constant size — the Season × Year interaction is significant (R² = 0.10, p = 0.011), and the per-year tests place the shift in all three years while making it much the largest in 2023 (R² = 0.21–0.24, p ≤ 0.009), intermediate in 2024 (R² = 0.10–0.14) and smallest in 2022 (R² = 0.09–0.10, p ≤ 0.010 under the full-rank distances; Tables S5b, S7c). What survives every distance measure is the ordering of the three years rather than the size of the gap between them: under the rank-3 robust Aitchison distance of panel A the 2022 contrast alone collapses to R² = 0.02 (p = 0.789), an artefact of the truncated distance rather than a year without a seasonal shift (Section 4.2, caveat iii). Panel B shows that this is a difference in where the seasons sit rather than in how tightly each clusters: summer samples are consistently further from their own centroid than winter samples in every year (Season p < 0.001), with no Season × Year interaction in that spread (p = 0.79). Two caveats belong with this figure and are treated in Section 4.2: summer samples are significantly more dispersed than winter ones, so part of the PERMANOVA R² reflects spread rather than location alone (Anderson 2001), and the axis percentages are shares of a rank-3 completion of the robust CLR matrix — panel A plots two of only three available dimensions, which is why its axis titles name that space rather than reporting a bare percentage (Section 4.1).

# UNITE taxonomy carries rank prefixes ("g__Thelebolus") and, at species level, a
# trailing hypothesis index ("intermedia 1"). Labels fall back down the ranks
# until something is assigned: a family name stands alone unambiguously, an order
# or class does not and is prefixed "unclassified". Markdown asterisks italicise
# real Latin names only -- never a rank name, never an Incertae sedis placeholder.
tax <- data.frame(as(tax_table(ps), "matrix"), stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
strip_rank <- function(x) sub("^[a-z]__", "", x)
clean_rank <- function(x) { x <- strip_rank(x)
  x[is.na(x) | x == "" | x == "NA" | grepl("unidentified", x, ignore.case = TRUE)] <- NA_character_
  x }
otu_label <- function(otu) {
  rk <- lapply(c("Genus", "Species", "Family", "Order", "Class"),
               function(r) clean_rank(tax[otu, r]))
  names(rk) <- c("Genus", "Species", "Family", "Order", "Class")
  incertae <- function(x) !is.na(x) && grepl("Incertae_sedis", x)
  nm <-
    if (!is.na(rk$Genus) && !incertae(rk$Genus)) {
      if (!is.na(rk$Species))
        paste0("*", rk$Genus, " ", sub("[ _]+[0-9]+$", "", rk$Species), "*")
      else paste0("*", rk$Genus, "*")
    } else if (incertae(rk$Genus))                      sub("_gen_Incertae_sedis", " (unclass.)", rk$Genus)
      else if (!is.na(rk$Family) && !incertae(rk$Family)) rk$Family
      else if (!is.na(rk$Order))                        paste("unclassified", rk$Order)
      else if (!is.na(rk$Class))                        paste("unclassified", rk$Class)
      else                                              "unclassified fungus"
  paste0(nm, " (", otu, ")")
}

# Every main figure is written three ways: PNG and JPG (600 dpi raster, for
# submission systems that demand one or the other) and PDF (vector, for print).
save_fig <- function(stem, obj, width, height, res = 600) {
  one <- function(open_dev) { open_dev(); on.exit(grDevices::dev.off()); print(obj) }
  one(function() grDevices::png(paste0(stem, ".png"), width = width, height = height,
                                units = "in", res = res))
  one(function() grDevices::jpeg(paste0(stem, ".jpg"), width = width, height = height,
                                 units = "in", res = res, quality = 95))
  pdf_dev <- if (capabilities("cairo")) grDevices::cairo_pdf else grDevices::pdf
  one(function() pdf_dev(paste0(stem, ".pdf"), width = width, height = height))
}

(A) Best-hit similarity of each OTU to its nearest UNITE Species Hypothesis, split into OTUs with no confident genus assignment (“dark”; n = 390 with a best hit) and those with one (“named”; n = 624). OTU1369 is labelled: it is winter’s single most abundant OTU at 11.2% of winter reads and is present in 20 of 26 winter droppings, yet its best UNITE hit is only 85% similar, so it forms a candidate new Species Hypothesis rather than matching anything named.

Roughly half of a typical winter dropping is reads that the canonical taxonomy cannot name (Section 3.4), and panel A shows that this single label covers two different problems. A little over a third of dark OTUs are near-identical to a UNITE reference and are dark only because minBoot = 80 declines to commit to a genus at that bootstrap — a cost of conservatism, and one that Species Hypothesis matching largely recovers. But a comparable fraction sits below 95% identity, where no threshold adjustment would help, and 228 of the 493 dark OTUs (46%) form candidate new Species Hypotheses rather than matching an existing one (panel B). The winter-abundant end of that distribution is what matters for this study: panel C shows that the dark OTUs carrying real winter read share are predominantly candidate-novel rather than placeable, and the most extreme of them, OTU1369, is simultaneously the most abundant and among the most prevalent OTUs in winter dung while being only 85% similar to its nearest named relative. Two caveats travel with the figure. “Candidate novel” is a statement about the reference database rather than a taxonomic claim: these are unimprinted clusters awaiting public sequence deposition and PlutoF registration (Section 3.4), so none of them yet carries a permanent identifier. 10.5 Figure 5 — A reproducible seasonal axis inside large among-individual variation (HMSC synthesis)

Figure 5: A joint species distribution model resolves a consistent seasonal axis at OTU level while showing how small it is against variation among individual droppings. (A) Per-OTU Season coefficient from the presence/absence (probit) HMSC model — positive = more likely to occur in summer — for the 25 most summer- and 25 most winter-shifted of the 226 modelled OTUs, coloured by functional guild, with 95% credible intervals; filled points are the 35 of these 50 whose interval excludes zero. The lower block gives each guild’s mean of the per-OTU estimates (diamond) with the interquartile range, over all OTUs in that guild rather than only the 50 plotted; these means reproduce the trait-level Gamma coefficients of Table S-HMSCd to within 0.04. (B) Mean share of per-OTU variance in CLR abundance attributed to Season, Year, the biological-sample random level and the PCR-replicate random level, for all 226 OTUs and within each guild (Table S-HMSCc). Panel A comes from the occurrence formulation and panel B from the abundance formulation, following the division of labour set out in Section 9.1: the probit model is the one whose per-OTU niches converge cleanly (Beta median PSRF 1.01, 99.6% of parameters below 1.1), while the variance partition is well behaved in the CLR model. The five guild classes are the model’s trait and differ slightly from the display split used in Figure 1 — 46 OTUs carrying a genus but no FUNGuild call sit in “Other assigned” here and in “Unassigned / dark” there (tables/guild_scheme_crosswalk.csv, Section 8.1).

Panel A makes the seasonal signal concrete at the level of individual lineages, and it is sharper in occurrence than in abundance: 55 of 226 OTUs have a Season coefficient whose 95% credible interval excludes zero, against 4 in the abundance model. The direction is overwhelmingly one-way — 157 of 226 OTUs shift towards summer, with a community median of +0.70 — so summer dung does not merely contain different fungi, it contains more kinds of fungi that are reliably present. The winter extreme is the familiar one: dark, taxonomically unresolved lineages (14 of the 25 most winter-shifted) together with pathotrophs, led by OTU1369, the most winter-shifted OTU in the community and the same unclassified Dothideomycete that dominates winter read share in Figure 4. The summer extreme, however, is not led by the coprophilous fungi. It is dominated by cold-adapted basidiomycete yeasts — Vishniacozyma, Mrakia, Piskurozyma, Phenoliferia — and by dark taxa, with Sporormiella (OTU636) the only dung saprotroph among the top 25. This is a real distinction rather than a contradiction of Figure 1: the coprophiles are what summer dung is made of in read share, but they are not what most reliably appears in summer, and the guild means in the lower block of panel A show the same ordering as the trait model (yeast-rich “other” +0.79, plant-associated +0.59, dung saprotroph +0.44, dark +0.37, pathotroph +0.07 — the only guild with no summer tendency). Panel B then supplies the scale that keeps all of this in proportion. Season and Year together account for about 20% of per-OTU variance (Season 11.2%, Year 8.4%), and the Season share is remarkably even across guilds (9.0% for plant-associated taxa to 14.3% for pathotrophs), while roughly half of the variance sits among biological samples — one dropping to the next — and a further third among PCR replicates of the same dropping. The community-mean Season share independently recovers the PERMANOVA R² of 0.11 from Section 4 with a completely different model. Taken together the two panels state the study’s central quantitative claim in one place: seasonal turnover is real, directional, and reproducible across methods, yet it is modest beside the idiosyncrasy of individual birds — which is why the model explains the samples it has seen (R² ≈ 0.46) but cannot predict one it has not (Section 9.7).

# Every main figure is written three ways: PNG and JPG (600 dpi raster, for
# submission systems that demand one or the other) and PDF (vector, for print).
save_fig <- function(stem, obj, width, height, res = 600) {
  one <- function(open_dev) { open_dev(); on.exit(grDevices::dev.off()); print(obj) }
  one(function() grDevices::png(paste0(stem, ".png"), width = width, height = height,
                                units = "in", res = res))
  one(function() grDevices::jpeg(paste0(stem, ".jpg"), width = width, height = height,
                                 units = "in", res = res, quality = 95))
  pdf_dev <- if (capabilities("cairo")) grDevices::cairo_pdf else grDevices::pdf
  one(function() pdf_dev(paste0(stem, ".pdf"), width = width, height = height))
}
