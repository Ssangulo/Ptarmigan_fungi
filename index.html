Section 2: Bioinformatics summary and study design

2.1 Study site and sampling design

Willow ptarmigan (Lagopus lagopus) faecal samples were collected at Lifjellet (Lierne municipality, Trøndelag, Norway) across a seasonal contrast: winter (March, snow-covered, browse-dominated diet) versus summer (June, green forage available) — replicated over three years (2022, 2023, 2024). The design is a Season × Year factorial, with repeated sampling of individually identifiable birds (microsatellite-confirmed where possible). Approximately 60 dung samples passed processing, of which ~31 have paired plant ITS2 diet data used for the H2 diet-richness mechanism (Section 7).

Each biological dung sample was amplified in two independent PCR replicates. DNA extraction, ITS2 amplification, demultiplexing (sabre), primer trimming (cutadapt), denoising (DADA2, non-pooled inference), and post-clustering curation (LULU) followed the workflow in Scripts/1_demultiplexing.R–Scripts/3_taxonomic_assignment.R; taxonomy was assigned against the UNITE reference (naïve Bayes, minBoot = 80). Negative, no-template, and extraction-blank controls were sequenced alongside samples and used for contaminant removal in Scripts/4_data_prep.R.

3.2 Coprophilous (dung-specialist) genera (E2)

This subsection addresses exploratory aim E2: documenting the occurrence and relative abundance of Sporormiella-type and other Sordariales taxa used in palynological herbivore-presence reconstructions, to provide a modern avian reference point for a proxy currently calibrated almost entirely on mammalian dung.

The canonical tax table was screened against a list of coprophilous genera comprising the standard palynological set — Sordaria, Podospora, Cercophora, Chaetomium, Schizothecium, Preussia, Sporormiella, Delitschia, Pilobolus, Ascobolus, Coprinopsis, Saccobolus, Sporormia — extended with four further dung-associated genera recovered here: Thelebolus, Coniochaeta, Psathyrella and Stropharia. This is the list used throughout this subsection, for both Table S3 and the read-share test below.

Six of the seventeen genera were detected, across 17 OTUs: Sporormiella (7 OTUs), Thelebolus (4), Psathyrella (2), Stropharia (2), and single OTUs of Coniochaeta and Coprinopsis. The eleven remaining genera — including Sordaria, Podospora and Cercophora, the taxa most often counted in palynological work — do not appear in this dataset at all. 3.3 Taxonomic assignment success by rank

Share of OTUs and reads given a confident name (UNITE naïve Bayes, minBoot = 80) at each rank on the canonical object. At genus rank this is the complement of the dark-taxa definition used in Section 3.4 (493 of 1129 OTUs without a confident genus). Assignment is near-complete to class and falls off from order downwards; species-level names are available for about a fifth of OTUs and reads. The per-OTU assignment rate is similar in the two seasons, but the per-sample read share assigned is consistently higher in summer from class to genus, because summer reads are concentrated in a few well-characterised coprophilous OTUs (Section 3.2) whereas winter reads fall disproportionately on unresolved lineages (Section 3.4).

H1 interpretation — the two clauses separate. Season is a significant predictor of community composition (PERMANOVA R² = 0.11, F = 6.92, p = 0.002), so the first clause of H1 (seasonal turnover exists) is supported. The second clause (magnitude consistent across years) is not: the Season × Year interaction is significant and substantial (R² = 0.10, F = 3.20, p = 0.011), meaning the size of the seasonal contrast differs between years. That interaction is significant under four of the five distance measures of Table S7b but only marginal under the zeros-retained robust CLR (p = 0.061; caveat iii), so the safe reading is that constancy of magnitude is not established, rather than that year-to-year differences are proven.

Table S5b localises that interaction. The seasonal shift is strong in 2023 (R² = 0.24, p = 0.005; between-season centroid distance 35.7), much weaker in 2024 (R² = 0.14, p = 0.127; distance 10.3) and effectively absent in 2022 (R² = 0.02, p = 0.789; distance 6.9) — though this last figure is the one part of the breakdown that is sensitive to the distance measure, see caveat (iii) below. The direction of the shift is the same in all three years — it is the magnitude that varies, dominated by an unusually well-separated 2023. This is exactly the pattern the functional-guild analysis finds independently (Section 8.3: dung-saprotroph share summer versus winter is significant in 2023, marginal in 2024 and not significant in 2022).

The interaction is a difference in location, not merely in spread: the distance-to-Season-centroid model (Table S7) finds a strong Season effect on within-season spread (p < 0.001) but no Season × Year interaction in that spread (p = 0.79). In other words, summer communities are consistently more heterogeneous than winter ones in every year, while how far apart the two seasons sit changes from year to year.

Caveats. (i) Beta-dispersion is significantly higher for summer than winter samples (F = 15.3, p = 0.002; Table S6), so part of the PERMANOVA R² reflects this difference in within-group spread rather than a pure centroid shift (Anderson 2001); this previews Section 5’s finding that summer communities are individually more dominance-skewed. (ii) With three years and 15–19 samples per year, a single atypical year moves the interaction substantially; the per-year tests in Table S5b are correspondingly low-powered (no year has more than 10 samples per season). The safe reading is “seasonal turnover is real and directionally consistent, but its magnitude is not established as constant across years” rather than a positive claim about year-specific ecology.

Under the three conventional full-rank measures (CLR with a pseudocount, Bray–Curtis, Jaccard) the 2022 contrast is modest but present (R² = 0.09–0.10, p ≤ 0.010); under the zeros-retained robust CLR it is of similar size but not resolved (R² = 0.07, p = 0.10), and the same holds for 2024 (R² = 0.09, p = 0.10), leaving only 2023 significant (R² = 0.14, p = 0.001; Table S7c). What survives every distance is the ordering — 2023 strongest (R² = 0.14–0.24), then 2024, then 2022 — and it is the size of the gap, not the ordering, that the rank-3 distance exaggerates. The defensible reading is that the seasonal shift was largest in 2023 and smaller in 2022 and 2024, where it is detected under most but not all distances: the data show neither that it was absent in 2022 nor that its magnitude was constant across years. Section 8.3’s independent guild result should be read the same way: it shares this ranking, but its non-significant 2022 cell is a question of power, not of a missing effect.

The two blocks are close to mutually exclusive. The ten winter-enriched OTUs account for 40.6% of the reads of an average winter sample but 3.1% of an average summer one; the ten summer-enriched OTUs account for 54.7% of summer but 6.1% of winter. Their cross-season detections are correspondingly sparse — a median of 3.5 of 26 summer samples for the winter-enriched set, and 1 of 26 winter samples for the summer-enriched set — and six of the twenty are never recovered in the opposite season at all. Across the whole object the same pattern holds at the level of bare presence: of 1,129 OTUs, only 171 (15%) are detected in both seasons, against 265 winter-only and 693 summer-only.

This is corroborated by the distance-based tests, which do not depend on the ranking above. Recomputing the identical Season * Year PERMANOVA on presence/absence alone — discarding every abundance — leaves the seasonal effect essentially intact (Jaccard R² = 0.074, p = 0.001; Sørensen R² = 0.113, p = 0.001) against R² = 0.086 for the full-rank abundance-based Aitchison distance. Two qualifications. The blocks are defined by relative read share, so a taxon that is genuinely present but rare in the other season can still appear here as near-absent; the claim these numbers support is that the dominant membership of the two seasons is largely disjoint, not that the seasons share no taxa. And the winter-only/summer-only counts above are inflated by rare OTUs seen in a single sample: restricting to OTUs detected in at least five samples of a season leaves 9 winter-only, 51 summer-only and 11 shared, which preserves the asymmetry but on much smaller numbers.

# Robust CLR WITHOUT matrix completion: log of the non-zero counts, centred on

Section 5: Diversity — H2

H2 predicts higher summer alpha diversity, tracking the richer summer diet. Two levels of analysis are presented together deliberately, because they appear to disagree in an informative way: the pooled-season estimate of 5.1 (which pools samples within a Season and asks about the Season’s total species pool) versus the per-sample estimate of 5.2 (which asks about diversity within a single dropping and rewards evenness). Both are computed with iNEXT3D, so the disagreement cannot be an artefact of using two different estimators — 5.1 standardises by coverage across the two pooled incidence sets, 5.2 standardises by size across the 52 individual samples. The disagreement is resolved by the dominance analysis in 5.3.

Per-sample Hill numbers are size-standardised to a common depth of 12,615 reads — the minimum library size — using iNEXT.3D::estimate3D(base = "size") on the raw counts. This returns the analytic expectation of the Hill number at that depth, rather than the value observed in one random rarefaction draw, so the estimate is deterministic and uses the full read depth of every sample. Coverage-based standardisation is deliberately not used at the per-sample level: estimated sample coverage is ≥ 0.99999 in all 52 samples (exactly 1 in 46 of them, since 43 contain no singleton OTUs at all), which leaves coverage-based and asymptotic estimators either undefined or equal to the observed richness, and would reintroduce the dependence on sequencing depth that standardisation is there to remove.

5.1 iNEXT3D: coverage-based taxonomic diversity

Incidence (presence in a dropping) depends on read depth, and summer libraries are about 2.6 times deeper than winter ones (median 551,236 vs 209,602 reads). The pooled comparison is therefore made with depth held equal: every sample is rarefied to the canonical minimum of 12,615 reads before the incidence matrices are built, both Seasons are compared at one common sample coverage (0.65), and the whole procedure is repeated over 50 independent rarefaction draws. Table S8 reports the median over draws of each estimate and of its bootstrap 95% CI. Asymptotic (extrapolated) estimates are not used for inference, because with 26 droppings per Season they extrapolate to roughly twice the observed richness.

5.3 Why the two metrics disagree: dominance and evenness

The crossover is not an artifact, and it is not a difference of estimator — both levels are iNEXT3D. Pooling a Season’s samples rewards the total species list (summer’s samples collectively contain more species, so summer wins at every q). Per-sample q1/q2 instead downweight rare taxa and reward within-sample evenness — and individual summer samples are far more dominance-skewed than winter ones, so a single summer sample’s effective diversity is lower even where its species list is longer.

Section 6: Lineage-specific seasonal responses — GLLVM (H1, OTU-resolved)

Sections 4 and 5 establish that the community shifts between seasons (H1) and that summer samples gain a long rare tail while becoming more dominance-skewed (H2). This section resolves H1 to the level of individual OTUs. We fit a generalized linear latent-variable model (GLLVM, negative-binomial) of per-OTU counts as a function of Season (winter as reference), with a library-size offset and random intercepts for individual bird and PCR replicate; the model is fitted on the full-complexity, one-row-per-PCR-replicate object (Scripts/7_gllvm.R, objects models/fit_nb_2.rds and models/fit_nb_2_2.rds). It answers which lineages drive the turnover — each with its own model uncertainty — and, through its latent variables, describes the residual co-occurrence that remains after Season is accounted for.

Warning

Read the per-OTU coefficients in this section as signs and ranks, not effect sizes. Two limitations apply (added 2026-09-29; wording to be finalised). (i) The models were fitted on the pre-depth-filter-correction object (57 samples, 114 replicate rows, 327 OTUs), not the canonical 52-sample object used elsewhere. (ii) Many Season coefficients are not estimable as finite effects: OTUs absent or nearly absent from one season push an unpooled fixed coefficient toward ±∞. 134 of the 327 OTUs are flagged for this (separation), and 101 of the 209 OTUs counted as significant are among them (the largest reaching β ≈ 1,295 on the log scale). Among the 193 unflagged OTUs, 106 still have |β| > 10, i.e. implausible fold changes (e¹⁰ ≈ 22,000), including the Sporormiella and Coniochaeta values quoted below (β ≈ 17). The OTU-level seasonal pattern is carried by HMSC in Section 9, which is fitted on the current data with shrinkage.

Section 7: Diet-tracking and Bayesian models — H2 and H3

7.1 Diet richness and fungal diversity — Bayesian mechanism (H2)

Section 5 established the coarse prediction of H2: summer communities are richer than winter ones, in step with the richer summer diet. Here we test the finer mechanism directly, on the samples that have paired plant ITS2 diet data and pass the 10,000-read depth filter of Sections 4–5 (n = 27 dung samples): does an individual sample’s fungal diversity track the richness of its own dietary plant community? Fungal Hill diversity is modelled as a function of standardised plant (diet) richness, adjusting for Season, in a Bayesian Gamma regression with a log link (brms). H2’s mechanism is supported if the plant-richness slope is positive with posterior probability P(slope > 0) > 0.95. Three further diet-matched winter samples (2,975–7,167 reads) are excluded here because the outcome is a per-sample diversity estimate standardised to a common depth of 12,615 reads (Section 5), and including them would force that depth down for every sample; the count models of Sections 7.2–7.3 retain them (n = 30) because they account for depth with a per-library offset instead.

The preregistration specified Year and individual bird as random intercepts. At this matched sample size that structure is unidentifiable — the 27 samples come from 24 distinct birds, so an individual random effect cannot be separated from residual variation, and Year spans only three levels — and the random-effects model does not converge. We therefore report two complementary, cleanly-converged fixed-effect models that bracket the estimate: a Season-adjusted model (plant_richness + Season + Year, Year as a fixed effect) and a more parsimonious drop-Year model (plant_richness + Season). Both model the raw Hill number with a Gamma likelihood and log link, the same family as the per-sample Season test of Table S9, with a weakly-informative prior on the slope (Normal(0, 1) per standard deviation of plant richness, on the log scale).

7.2 OTU–plant covariation (H3) - H3 not supported - still working

Section 7.1 asked whether overall fungal diversity tracks overall diet richness. H3 is finer-grained and taxon-resolved: does each fungal OTU covary with particular diet-plant taxa, and — the preregistered contrast — do recognised coprophilous taxa show the diffuse, non-specific plant associations expected of dung specialists dispersed independently of any host plant, while other taxa may show the tight, single-plant covariation that would flag a likely ingested endophyte passenger? Inference is strictly forward-directional: a tight single-plant association is read as evidence of a likely ingested passenger; a diffuse or absent association is not read as evidence of gut/dung residency.

Model. We fit a single hierarchical hurdle negative-binomial model (brms) to the diet-matched subset. To retain as many matched samples as possible we use the PCR-replicate-level, minimally depth-filtered object (mirroring the GLLVM of Section 6) rather than the collapsed, 10,000-read-filtered object of Sections 4–5; this recovers three additional low-depth winter dung samples that carry paired plant data, raising the matched subset to n = 30 biological samples (60 PCR-replicate libraries; 19 winter, 11 summer). Forty-six OTUs present in ≥5 of the 30 samples are modelled. The hurdle model has two parts: whether an OTU is detected in a library (occurrence) and how abundant it is when detected (abundance given presence). Both parts carry Season, Year and four diet-plant predictors, each with a community-wide coefficient and a per-OTU deviation (partial pooling); the abundance part also has a per-replicate log-library-size offset and a per-OTU biological-sample random effect, so the two PCR replicates of a sample are treated as replication rather than independent samples. The preregistered “individual” random effect is replaced by the sample effect for the same reason as in Section 7.1. The diet predictors are the four plant taxa present as ≥1% of diet reads in at least seven matched samples — Betula (resolved only to genus), Vaccinium myrtillus, V. uliginosum and Empetrum nigrum — each as a centred log-ratio of its share of the diet (zero reads set to 0.1% of the diet), standardised. Occurrence slopes are reported on the detection scale: positive means the OTU is detected more often as that plant rises in the diet. Because a slope shared by every OTU is not specificity, an OTU–plant association counts as resolved when the OTU’s own deviation from the community-wide slope has a 95% credible interval excluding zero. Per-OTU specificity is summarised, separately for each part, by a Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) of the |plant slopes| (0.25 if spread evenly across the four plants, 1 if concentrated on one), and the preregistered contrast compares it between FUNGuild-flagged coprophilous OTUs and plant-associated (endophyte/phyllosphere/plant-saprotroph) OTUs. The model converged cleanly (no divergent transitions, maximum R̂ = 1.004, minimum bulk ESS 1,034) and reproduces the observed mean count and the per-OTU fraction of zeros (r = 0.99 between observed and predicted); its negative-binomial tail predicts occasional counts above the largest library size.

7.3 OTU–plant covariation — GLLVM cross-check (H3)

To check that the H3 verdict of Section 7.2 is not an artefact of one modelling framework, we repeat the analysis with a generalised linear latent-variable model (GLLVM) — the same model-based framework used for the OTU-resolved H1 in Section 6, now with the diet-plant predictors added. Everything about the data is held identical to the Bayesian model: the same PCR-replicate-level matched subset (n = 30 biological samples, 60 libraries; 46 OTUs present in ≥5 samples), the same four standardised diet-plant predictors (Section 7.2), the same per-library log-size offset, and the same specificity index and preregistered coprophilous-vs-plant-associated contrast. Only the estimator differs, which is the point: agreement across two very different fitting machineries is stronger evidence than either alone.

Model. Each OTU’s counts are modelled with a negative-binomial GLLVM (gllvm), with per-OTU (species-specific) fixed coefficients for Season, Year and each diet plant, a biological-sample random row effect (so the two PCR replicates of a sample count as replication, not independent samples), and the log-library-size offset. gllvm has no hurdle family, so each slope here mixes occurrence and abundance. We fit 0, 1 and 2 residual latent variables and select by AIC; here num.lv = 0 is preferred — beyond the sample random effect the latent variables capture no further residual co-occurrence (so, unlike Section 6, there is no residual ordination to show). One difference from the Bayesian model is forced by the software: the natural partial-pooling analog (gllvm’s randomX species-specific random slopes) is silently dropped on this dataset, returning no plant-slope parameters at all. We therefore estimate the plant slopes as fixed per-OTU coefficients with Wald standard errors — an unpooled frequentist estimate that brackets the Bayesian pooled one from the other side. Rare on/off OTUs push their fixed coefficients toward ±∞ (near-separation); as in Section 6 these are flagged (15 of 46 OTUs) and excluded from the specificity ranking, contrast and plots, leaving 31 well-estimated OTUs. Specificity is the same Herfindahl index of the |plant slopes|; a resolved single-plant association here means the 90 % Wald interval of an OTU’s strongest plant slope excludes zero.

Warning

Caveat on the per-OTU slopes. Even among the 31 retained OTUs the unpooled slopes are very large for a per-SD predictor (median |slope| 3.7, 90th percentile 14.0, maximum 28.8 on the log scale) — larger than with the six-genus predictors used until 2026-09, because each OTU now carries its own Season and Year coefficients on 30 samples. The count of Wald-resolved associations in this section therefore overstates what is estimable; the pooled Bayesian model of Section 7.2 resolves 10 OTU-specific associations, all in occurrence. What this section can support is the model-level verdict and the direction of the associations (Table S20b), not the individual slope sizes.

Section 8: Functional guilds — FUNGuild

Sections 3–6 characterise the seasonal shift taxonomically — which lineages turn over between winter and summer. This section asks what that community does: each OTU is assigned to a functional guild, and the guild composition of the dung mycobiome is compared across seasons. It is the descriptive counterpart to Section 9, which puts the same trait → season link inside a joint species distribution model. The two are deliberately built on the same classification rule so their results are directly comparable, but they differ in scope: Section 8 works on read shares across all 1129 OTUs of the canonical 52-sample object (no prevalence filter, because this is a description of where the reads go), whereas Section 9 models the 226 OTUs that pass its prevalence threshold. All results here are produced by Scripts/8_functional_guilds.R.

8.1 Guild assignment and the classification rule

Guild annotation uses FUNGuild (Nguyen et al. 2016), run through its command-line tool against each OTU’s own taxonomy string, so that assignments are made at the finest rank available per OTU (species, genus, family or order) rather than by a single-rank lookup. 693 of the 1129 canonical OTUs (61.4%) receive a guild call at a usable confidence ranking; the remaining 436 return no assignment — either because no matching taxonomy record exists in the FUNGuild database or because the match carries no ranked guild.

FUNGuild returns compound guild strings rather than a single label — for example |Dung Saprotroph|-Endophyte-Plant Saprotroph-Undefined Saprotroph. OTUs are collapsed to a single class by a priority rule (first match wins): dung saprotroph → plant-associated → pathotroph, with anything else falling to other assigned or unassigned (no FUNGuild call at all). Coprophily takes precedence deliberately: it is the guild the study is about, and it would otherwise be absorbed into a generic saprotroph category. An OTU counts as a dung saprotroph if FUNGuild lists Dung Saprotroph or its genus is one of fourteen recognised coprophilous genera (the Section 3.2 list, omitting Sporormia, Psathyrella and Stropharia). That difference is immaterial here: Sporormia is absent from the dataset, both Stropharia OTUs are already assigned by their FUNGuild string, and the two Psathyrella OTUs carry 0.04% of reads, so no guild share below changes at the reported precision. This is the identical rule used for the Section 9 guild trait.

Interpretation and caveats.

What this adds. The guild layer reproduces the seasonal signal independently of taxonomy and gives it a functional reading: summer dung supports an actively coprophilous community, winter dung does not. Section 9 tests the same link within a joint model and reaches a concordant conclusion — guild predicts seasonal occurrence, with dung saprotrophs more likely to occur in summer (posterior support 0.96) — so the descriptive and model-based views agree.

Relation to the dark taxa (E1). The winter unassigned share here (51.1%) closely tracks the dark-taxa share reported in Section 3.4 (51.3% of winter reads), but the two are not the same set: “unassigned” here means no FUNGuild annotation (436 OTUs), while “dark” in Section 3.4 means no confident genus in the canonical taxonomy (493 OTUs). They share 390 OTUs, and 103 OTUs lack a genus yet still receive a FUNGuild call at family or order level. The definitions coincide in winter but diverge in summer (21.0% unassigned versus 31.2% dark), so the two figures should not be used interchangeably. Section 3.4 establishes that a substantial part of this winter fraction is genuinely novel rather than merely absent from reference databases; the functional consequence is that winter guild composition is the least well characterised part of this dataset, and its apparent depletion of coprophiles is partly a statement about annotation coverage.

Limitations. (i) Read share is not biomass: PCR and copy-number biases mean these proportions describe sequence abundance, not fungal abundance. (ii) FUNGuild assignments are database-derived and inherit its taxonomic and geographic biases; assignments made at family or order level are necessarily coarser than species-level ones, and confidence rankings (Table S-GUILDb does not stratify by them) are Probable for the majority of assigned OTUs. (iii) The compositional constraint discussed above means that a guild’s decline in relative terms need not imply any absolute decline. (iv) A guild label describes an ecological capability inferred from relatives, not a verified activity in this substrate.

Section 9: Joint species distribution model — HMSC (H1 synthesis)

Sections 4–6 treat the seasonal signal one lens at a time: community dissimilarity (H1), per-sample diversity (H2), and per-OTU differential abundance (GLLVM). This section brings them into a single hierarchical model. We fit a joint species distribution model (HMSC — Hierarchical Modelling of Species Communities; Scripts/10_hmsc.R) that simultaneously (i) partitions each OTU’s variance among Season, Year, and the nested sampling structure; (ii) asks whether an OTU’s functional guild predicts how it responds to Season (the trait → environment link); (iii) recovers each OTU’s seasonal niche for a like-for-like cross-check against the Section-6 GLLVM; (iv) estimates the residual co-occurrence left once Season, Year and traits are accounted for; and (v) tests for phylogenetic signal in seasonal niches. It is the model-based synthesis of the H1 story, fitted directly on the corrected canonical data (unlike the Section-6 GLLVM, which predates the depth-filter fix).

9.1 Model, design, and what is (and isn’t) trustworthy

The models use the one-row-per-PCR-replicate object (alldat_full$nopool; 109 replicate rows from 55 biological dung samples), retaining the 226 OTUs present in ≥ 5 replicates — the same prevalence rule used for the GLLVM/H3 analyses. Fixed effects are Season (winter as reference) + Year; the species trait is the five-level functional guild class derived from FUNGuild (dung saprotroph, plant-associated, pathotroph, dark/unassigned, other — assigned by the priority rule in Scripts/10_hmsc.R, counts below). Three models are fitted:

  • Headline — CLR abundance, replicates averaged. Each PCR replicate is CLR-transformed (the compositional normalisation that stands in for a library-size offset), and the two replicates of a dropping are then averaged, giving one row per dropping (55 rows). The random level is the biological sample (the dropping), with two latent factors. This model carries the abundance results of this section and both panels of Figure 3.
  • Complement — CLR abundance, replicate level. The same CLR response with both replicates kept as rows (109 rows), and two random levels: biological sample (two latent factors) and PCR replicate (one latent factor).
  • Robustness — presence/absence (probit), replicate level, with biological sample and PCR replicate as random levels.

Both CLR models scale each OTU’s response to unit variance before fitting and cap the number of latent factors; the reason for the cap, and for the choice of headline, is given below Table S-HMSCb.

An individual-bird random level was deliberately not included. Microsatellite genotyping succeeded for only a handful of repeat birds, so a bird level (with unidentified droppings entered as singletons) would have ~51 units against 55 biological samples — effectively one-to-one with the sample level, confounding the two sets of latent factors. In the three-level version this stopped the CLR community-level parameters from converging and made the variance partition unstable, so the bird level was dropped, leaving sample + PCR (mirroring the GLLVM’s (1 | Sample_ID_field)). Dropping it was not by itself sufficient; see the note below Table S-HMSCb. Each model was fitted with four chains (1000 samples, thinning 50, 25,000 transient iterations).

Why the replicate-averaged model is the headline. An earlier version of this section used a single CLR model at the replicate level with an uncapped number of latent factors (seven at the sample level, five at the PCR level). Its four chains converged to four different answers (Table S-HMSCb2): the Thelebolus Season coefficient was 0.36, 3.03, 0.69 and −0.03 in the four chains, and the Season share of variance between 5% and 28%. The PSRF understated the problem because each chain mixed well within its own solution. The cause is structural. Season is constant within a dropping, so enough sample-level latent factors can reproduce the Season contrast themselves, and each chain divided the seasonal signal differently between the Season coefficients and the latent factors. Longer chains do not cure this; capping the number of latent factors does, and both CLR models reported here are capped.

With the cap, both CLR models converge on the Season effects (Table S-HMSCb). They differ in how they treat the two PCR replicates of each dropping. The replicate-level model estimates PCR replicate as its own random level, but the split of variance between dropping and replicate rests on only 54 replicate pairs and is weakly identified: in an earlier independent fit with the identical specification, its chains settled in two different splits (dropping/PCR 0.52/0.14 in two chains, 0.43/0.27 in the other two), whereas in the fit reported here the chains agree (Table S-HMSCc3). The replicate-level model also counts the two replicates as partly independent observations, which narrows its credible intervals (median width 0.84 of the headline model’s; Table S-HMSCc4). Averaging the replicates removes both issues by construction — one row per dropping, no replicate-level split to identify — and its chains agree to within 0.003 on every reported guild mean and variance component.

The two models agree on what the figures report: per-OTU Season coefficients correlate at Pearson r = 0.97 (Spearman ρ = 0.89), and 56 OTUs have a 95% credible interval excluding zero in both (Table S-HMSCc4). The replicate-level model is therefore kept as the complement that carries an explicit PCR-replicate level, and the PCR-replicate variance the headline model averages away is measured directly in Section 9.2 (Table S-HMSCc2).

The probit (occurrence) model was not refitted. Its PSRFs are low (Beta median 1.01, 99.6% below 1.1), but compared chain by chain its guild means differ by up to 0.24 on the probit scale — the same qualitative answer in every chain, with looser agreement than the CLR models. That agreement is also partly a property of this particular fit: refitted from scratch, and again with its latent factors capped as in the CLR models, the same model mixes less well, with chains up to about 0.6 apart on a guild mean, chiefly for the common, strongly seasonal OTUs. Every refit returns the same answers — per-OTU Season coefficients correlate at r ≥ 0.99 with the fit reported here, and every guild-level direction is unchanged — so the probit results below should be read for their direction and ranking, not their exact size.

9.2 Variance partitioning: a reproducible seasonal axis within large individual heterogeneity

For every OTU the headline model decomposes the variance it explains in CLR abundance into contributions from Season, Year, and the biological-sample (dropping) random level. Averaged across OTUs, Season accounts for 31.5% and Year for 18.5%, with the remaining 49.9% carried by differences among droppings. These are shares of the variance the model explains, not of total variance, so they are not comparable with a PERMANOVA R².

The ecological reading is two-sided but coherent. The seasonal axis is real and reproducible, yet at the level of an individual OTU the largest single component is still how much dung communities differ from one dropping to the next. HMSC formalises what the ordination and dominance analyses already suggested: season shifts the community in a consistent direction, on top of large individual-level variability. This decomposition, broken down by guild exactly as in Table S-HMSCc, is Figure 3B of the main text (Section 10.3).

PCR-replicate variance. Because the headline model averages the two PCR replicates of each dropping, it has no PCR level to partition. The replicate-to-replicate variance is measured instead, outside the model, from the 54 droppings with two replicates (Table S-HMSCc2). Both replicates come from the same DNA extract, so any difference between them is measurement noise. For each OTU, half the mean squared difference between the two replicates’ CLR values estimates the replicate variance; divided by the OTU’s total CLR variance and averaged over OTUs, replicate noise accounts for 32% of CLR variance (bootstrap 95% CI 28–40%; median over OTUs 25%). The estimate does not depend on the method: dividing instead by the variance left after Season and Year gives 36%, and a per-OTU mixed model with a dropping random intercept gives 34% (per-OTU correlation with the former 0.998). Nor is it driven by unequal sequencing depth: the two replicates differ in depth by a median factor of 1.31, and the size of that difference does not predict how different the replicates look (Spearman ρ = 0.21, p = 0.13, n = 54). It is not spread evenly, however. Coprophilous dung saprotrophs are the most repeatable guild (17%, CI 11–25%) and pathotrophs the least (50%, CI 36–62%).

The replicate noise is almost entirely detection, not abundance. Where both replicates detect an OTU, their CLR values agree closely: replicate noise is then a median 3% of variance. But in 46% of the droppings in which an OTU is found, only one of the two replicates detects it — the signature of low-abundance taxa near the detection limit, where PCR and sequencing sampling decide whether a few reads appear. Coprophiles are detected in both replicates more consistently (only one replicate detects them in 32% of cases) and, when both detect them, differ by a median 1% of variance: where Thelebolus or Sporormiella is present, both replicates see it at similar levels. The complementary replicate-level model, which fits PCR replicate as a random level, gives a comparable picture: 27% of its explained variance at the PCR level, 43% at the dropping level, and a Season share of 17.5% (Table S-HMSCc3). Its Season share is lower than the headline model’s because replicate noise, which the headline model averages away, enters its denominator.

9.3 Guild predicts seasonal occurrence — the coprophilous summer signature, formalised

The trait → environment matrix (Gamma) asks whether an OTU’s guild predicts its seasonal response. Read from the well-converged probit model, it does. Relative to the winter reference, dung saprotrophs are markedly more likely to occur in summer (the reference-guild Season effect is positive with posterior support 0.96), and the yeast-rich “other” group shows the same direction (support 0.83); pathotrophs lean the opposite way, toward winter (support 0.85 in the negative direction). Plant-associated and dark/unassigned taxa show no clear guild-level seasonal preference.

On the abundance scale the same link can now be read from the headline model, whose Gamma converges (Table S-HMSCb), and it is sharper (Table S-HMSCd2). The dung-saprotroph guild’s own Season effect is positive (+0.31 in standardised units, 95% CrI [0.07, 0.54], support 0.994). This is the guild-level counterpart to the taxonomic and dominance results (Sections 3.2, 5.4): the recognised coprophilous fungi — Sporormiella, Thelebolus, Coniochaeta — are not a fixed cold-adapted core but a summer-occurring dung guild, consistent with the richer, more fungal-propagule-laden summer diet. HMSC states that pattern as a trait effect estimated across all 226 OTUs at once, rather than genus by genus.

9.4 Per-OTU seasonal niches, and agreement with the GLLVM

The same per-OTU niche can be estimated on the occurrence scale, with the probit model. There, 55 of the 226 OTUs have a Season coefficient whose 95% credible interval excludes zero, against 74 in the headline abundance model. The two formulations rank the community’s seasonal responses consistently (Spearman ρ = 0.76), and the probit per-OTU estimates aggregate to the guild-level Gamma of Section 9.3 to within 0.04, so nothing here contradicts the results above. What the occurrence scale does change is which taxa sit at the summer extreme. In abundance the coprophiles lead outright; in occurrence the shift is community-wide (157 of 226 OTUs point towards summer, median +0.70) and the extreme is occupied by cold-adapted basidiomycete yeasts — Vishniacozyma, Mrakia, Piskurozyma, Phenoliferia — with Sporormiella (OTU636) the only dung saprotroph in the top 25 and the mean dung-saprotroph rank falling to 125 of 226 (from 59 in abundance). The distinction is ecologically substantive rather than a modelling artefact: coprophilous fungi are what summer dung is largely made of in read share, but they are not the taxa that most reliably appear in summer. 9.6 Phylogenetic signal in seasonal niches

Adding the OTU phylogeny (the maximum-likelihood ITS2 tree of Scripts/6_diversity_analyses.R, pruned to the 226 modelled OTUs) to the headline model estimates ρ: the share of the OTU-to-OTU variation in responses, left after the guild trait, that follows the phylogeny under a Brownian-motion model. The chains converge and agree on ρ (Table S-HMSCb, Table S-HMSCf), and adding the phylogeny leaves the per-OTU niches and the guild link essentially unchanged.

ρ is effectively 1. Its posterior median is 0.985 (95% CrI [0.950, 0.995]), and the posterior rises steadily toward the top of the grid, with a quarter of all draws on its highest point. That upper limit is set by the grid, not the data: ρ = 1 itself cannot be fitted, because OTUs with near-identical sequences have near-zero branch lengths and the phylogenetic covariance becomes singular. Under the model, essentially all of the variation in responses left after guild is phylogenetically structured. This replaces the ρ ≈ 0.72 reported from the earlier, non-converged model structure (Section 9.1), whose uncapped latent factors absorbed part of the same variation. Note that ρ applies to an OTU’s responses jointly — baseline abundance, Season and Year together.

A single ρ does not say at which scale relatives resemble each other, and it does not isolate Season. Table S-HMSCf2 therefore correlates the Season responses of every pair of OTUs, binned by their distance in the tree. It uses the headline model’s coefficients, which contain no phylogeny, so the comparison is not circular.

Close relatives respond alike, and the resemblance reaches well beyond near-identical sequences. The explanatory fit is moderate (community-mean R² ≈ 0.29 for the headline abundance model) but cross-validated R² is near zero (≈ 0.03) — the model does not predict the fungal community of a dropping it has not seen. Far from undercutting the story, this is the quantitative statement of it: the seasonal and guild effects documented above are real and directional but small against the dominant, idiosyncratic variation among individual droppings. A ptarmigan’s dung mycobiome is shaped by season in a reproducible way, yet remains largely individual — which is exactly what a transient, diet- and propagule-sourced community should look like, and a useful caution for anyone hoping to read season directly off a single sample.

In summary, HMSC does not overturn any earlier result; it unifies them and adds two things the single-lens analyses could not: a variance decomposition that sizes the seasonal signal against individual heterogeneity, and a formal trait- and phylogeny-level account of why the seasonal turnover looks the way it does — a summer-occurring coprophilous dung guild, phylogenetically conserved, riding on top of large among-individual variability.

Figure 3: A joint species distribution model places the seasonal axis at OTU level and sizes it against the variation among individual droppings. Both panels come from one abundance (CLR) HMSC model fitted to 226 OTUs in 55 droppings, with each dropping’s two PCR replicates averaged, Season and Year as fixed effects, and the dropping as random level (Section 9.1). The model’s own guild-level estimate is the trait-level Gamma (Section 9.3, Table S-HMSCd2). The four MCMC chains agree on every plotted estimate (Beta PSRF ≤ 1.01; Table S-HMSCb). (B) Mean share of per-OTU explained variance attributed to Season, Year and the dropping, for all 226 OTUs and then within each guild (Table S-HMSCc). The italic right-hand column is not part of that partition. It is the share of each OTU’s total CLR variance that lies between the two PCR replicates of a dropping, measured outside the model (Table S-HMSCc2), and it mostly reflects rare taxa detected by one replicate but not the other. The three highlighted rows are drawn larger because they are the groups this study is about. The five guild classes are the model’s trait and differ slightly from the display split used in Figure 1: 46 OTUs carrying a genus but no FUNGuild call sit in “Other assigned” here and in “Unassigned / dark” there (tables/guild_scheme_crosswalk.csv, Section 8.1).

Panel A makes the seasonal signal concrete at the level of individual lineages, and it does so among the taxa that actually constitute the community: the 22 OTUs plotted are every OTU holding at least 1% of modelled read share, and they carry 73% of the modelled reads between them. Read from the top, the panel is the study’s taxonomic result in one column. Measured separately, a further 32% of total CLR variance is replicate-to-replicate noise within a dropping, most of it whether rare taxa are detected at all; coprophiles are the most repeatable guild (17%). Taken together the panels state the central quantitative claim in one place: seasonal turnover is real, directional and consistent across methods, yet it sits beside large idiosyncrasy among individual droppings — which is why the model explains the samples it has seen (R² ≈ 0.29) but predicts held-out droppings far less well (cross-validated R² ≈ 0.03; Section 9.7).

Figure 4: The summer dung mycobiome is the richer assemblage when droppings are pooled, yet each individual dropping is far less even. (A) Taxonomic diversity of each season’s pooled sample set at Hill orders q = 0, 1 and 2, at equal sequencing depth (every sample rarefied to 12,615 reads) and equal sample coverage (0.65) (iNEXT3D, incidence data); points are the median estimate over 50 rarefaction draws, bars the median 95% confidence interval, and the grey segment joins the two seasons within an order. Summer exceeds winter at every order (Table S8). (B) Per-sample Hill numbers for the same 52 samples, size-standardised with iNEXT3D to a common depth of 12,615 reads, drawn in the same three columns against the same season axis so that the two rows can be read against one another; boxes show the median and interquartile range, points are individual samples, white diamonds are season means, and the grey segment joins those means. The slope that rises in all three columns of (A) rises only at q = 0 here and falls at q = 1 and q = 2 — that reversal is the crossover. Annotations give the year-adjusted summer/winter ratio and its p-value from a gamma GLM fitted to the plotted per-sample values (Table S9); the q = 0 contrast depends on the model family (p = 0.026–0.29; Table S9b), the q = 1 and q = 2 contrasts do not. Both rows scale y freely within each column, because the three Hill orders differ by an order of magnitude. (C) The mechanism: per-sample top-1-OTU read share and Pielou evenness J, both on a 0–1 scale, with symbols as in (B) and brackets giving the Wilcoxon rank-sum result (Table S10). (D) The identity of the dominants — the five OTUs with the highest mean within-season read share in each season, coloured by functional guild under the rule of Section 8.1. Bar labels give that mean share and, in parentheses, the number of the season’s 26 samples in which the OTU was detected at all. Taxa are named at the finest confidently assigned rank, so an OTU with no genus appears under its family, or as unclassified plus its order or class. Candida parapsilosis is coloured plant-associated because its compound FUNGuild string contains Epiphyte; the rule is applied unchanged rather than special-cased, so that Figures 1 and 4 and Sections 8–9 classify every OTU identically.

Panels A and B put the same question to two scales and get opposite answers, and that disagreement is the result rather than a problem with it. Pooled across a season and with sequencing depth held equal, summer is the richer assemblage at every Hill order — 548 against winter’s 383 OTUs at q = 0, and the gap widens under abundance weighting (412 vs 261 at q = 1; 287 vs 162 at q = 2). Within a single dropping the ordering reverses as soon as abundance enters: winter droppings are significantly more diverse at q = 1 (14.9 vs 7.4 effective OTUs; summer/winter 0.46, p = 0.001) and q = 2 (10.1 vs 4.6; 0.40, p < 0.001), whereas at q = 0 summer’s higher mean (48.3 vs 31.3) is a weak, family-dependent difference carried by a few very rich summer samples (Tables S9, S9b). Panel C locates the reversal in dominance rather than in the species lists: an average summer sample gives 56% of its reads to a single OTU against 25% in winter, and Pielou evenness falls from 0.72 to 0.38 (both p < 0.001). Summer droppings are far more top-heavy, which is precisely what depresses q = 1 and q = 2 even where a dropping’s species list is as long as, or longer than, a winter one. Panel D names the taxa responsible, and they are the ones a coprophilous account predicts: Thelebolus, Coniochaeta and Sporormiella intermedia together take 44% of summer reads and are all dung saprotrophs, whereas winter’s leading OTUs are mostly unclassified below class and carry no guild call at all — among them OTU1369, an unclassified Dothideomycete that is winter’s most abundant OTU and is present in 20 of 26 winter samples, and which is taken up as a dark taxon in Section 3.4. Two caveats travel with the figure. The per-sample richness contrast in (B) is not robust to the choice of model family (Table S9b), so the crossover is firm at q = 1 and q = 2 and in the pooled estimates of (A), but not as a per-dropping richness gain. And the shares in (D) are seasonal averages, not universals — Thelebolus reaches its 24.9% mean while being detected in only 15 of the 26 summer droppings.

# trailing hypothesis index ("intermedia 1"). Labels fall back down the ranks

clean_rank <- function(x) { x <- strip_rank(x)
  x[is.na(x) | x == "" | x == "NA" | grepl("unidentified", x, ignore.case = TRUE)] <- NA_character_

otu_label <- function(otu) {
  rk <- lapply(c("Genus", "Species", "Family", "Order", "Class"),
               function(r) clean_rank(tax[otu, r]))
  names(rk) <- c("Genus", "Species", "Family", "Order", "Class")

  nm <-
    if (!is.na(rk$Genus) && !incertae(rk$Genus)) {
      if (!is.na(rk$Species))
        paste0("*", rk$Genus, " ", sub("[ _]+[0-9]+$", "", rk$Species), "*")
      else paste0("*", rk$Genus, "*")
    } else if (incertae(rk$Genus))                      sub("_gen_Incertae_sedis", " (unclass.)", rk$Genus)
      else if (!is.na(rk$Family) && !incertae(rk$Family)) rk$Family
      else if (!is.na(rk$Order))                        paste("unclassified", rk$Order)
      else if (!is.na(rk$Class))                        paste("unclassified", rk$Class)
      else                                              "unclassified fungus"
  paste0(nm, " (", otu, ")")

10.5 Figure 5 — Dark, largely novel lineages dominate winter dung (E1)

Figure 5: Winter dung is dominated by fungal lineages that carry no confident genus, and most of that fraction is not merely unnamed but absent from the reference database. (A) UNITE Species Hypothesis placement of all 493 dark OTUs — those the naïve-Bayes classifier leaves without a confident genus. The second and third categories are the same kind of outcome: neither falls inside an existing named Species Hypothesis, and they differ only in whether the sequence has any UNITE relative at all. “Candidate novel SH” has one (median best hit 92.0% identity across these 228 OTUs) and so inherits a partial lineage from its neighbours; “no reference within 80% id” has none, and additionally absorbs 13 OTUs that the pipeline filtered before the matching step. Together they are 331 of the 493 dark OTUs (67%). (B) Mean per-sample read share of the same categories, by season, with the named fraction added to reach 100% of reads (52 droppings, 26 per season; segments below 5% are unlabelled). This is the read-weighted counterpart to (A): counting OTUs says nothing about how much of a dropping they are. Candidate-novel lineages hold 29% of winter reads against 7% of summer reads (Wilcoxon p = 1.9 × 10⁻⁵), and the dark fraction as a whole is 51% of winter against 31% of summer reads. (C) The 30 dark OTUs with the highest mean winter read share (Table S-E1b), plotted as prevalence — the number of the 26 winter samples with any detection — against that mean share, coloured as in (A). OTU1369 is labelled: it is winter’s single most abundant OTU at 11.2% of winter reads and is present in 20 of 26 winter droppings, yet its best UNITE hit is only 85% similar, so it forms a candidate new Species Hypothesis rather than matching anything named.

Roughly half of a typical winter dropping is reads that the canonical taxonomy cannot name (Section 3.4), and that single label covers two different problems. A little over a third of dark OTUs are near-identical to a UNITE reference and are dark only because minBoot = 80 declines to commit to a genus at that bootstrap — a cost of conservatism, and one that Species Hypothesis matching largely recovers (of the 390 dark OTUs with a best hit at all, 37% sit at ≥ 99% identity; Figure S-E1a). But a comparable fraction of them (38%) sits below 95% identity, where no threshold adjustment would help, and panel A shows where that leads: 228 of the 493 dark OTUs (46%) form candidate new Species Hypotheses rather than matching an existing one, and a further 103 (21%) have no UNITE reference within 80% identity at all. Two thirds of the unnamed community is therefore unnamed because the reference lacks it, not because the classifier was cautious. Panel B supplies the weight behind that count, and it is strongly seasonal: candidate-novel lineages account for 29% of winter reads but only 7% of summer reads, so winter dung is not merely more dark than summer dung, it is dark in a different way — dominated by lineages with no named counterpart rather than by unnamed relatives of known ones. Panel C locates that signal in individual taxa: the dark OTUs carrying real winter read share are predominantly candidate-novel, and the most extreme of them, OTU1369, is simultaneously the most abundant and among the most prevalent OTUs in winter dung while being only 85% similar to its nearest named relative. One caveat travels with the figure throughout. “Candidate novel” is a statement about the reference database rather than a taxonomic claim: these are unimprinted clusters awaiting public sequence deposition and PlutoF registration (Section 3.4), so none of them yet carries a permanent identifier.

# Season is capitalised for display only; winter first because the figure's
# subject is the winter community.

                    levels = c("Winter", "Summer"))
bar <- aggregate(read_share ~ Season + cat, data = rs, FUN = mean)
bar$pct <- 100 * bar$read_share
bar <- bar[order(bar$Season, bar$cat), ]
# Label positions are computed here rather than left to position_stack. The text
# layer plots a SUBSET (segments too thin to hold a label), and position_stack
# re-stacks whatever data it is given -- so the two layers would silently drift
# apart in any season where a segment is dropped. geom_col stacks the first
# factor level on top, hence the descending cumulative.
bar$ymid <- unlist(lapply(split(bar$pct, bar$Season), function(v)
  (sum(v) - cumsum(c(0, head(v, -1)))) - v / 2))
# Drift guard: these means must reproduce the per-OTU table's season columns,
# which are computed independently (script 9 Section 4, not 4b).

                       "Named (genus assigned)"), levels = STRAT_LEVELS)),
              function(i) sum(sh$winter_mean_share[i]), numeric(1))
win <- setNames(bar$read_share[bar$Season == "Winter"],
                as.character(bar$cat[bar$Season == "Winter"]))
stopifnot(max(abs(chk[STRAT_LEVELS] - win[STRAT_LEVELS])) < 1e-6)

nov <- rs[rs$cat == "Candidate novel SH", ]

  scale_y_continuous(breaks = seq(0, 100, 25), expand = expansion(mult = c(0, 0.02))) +
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(0, 100)) +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Mean per-sample read share (%)",

  base_theme +
  # The figure's one legend lives here: panel B is the only one that carries all
  # four levels, and its geom_col draws square keys, which read correctly for

  guides(fill = guide_legend(order = 1, nrow = 1))

# Every main figure is written three ways: PNG and JPG (600 dpi raster, for
# submission systems that demand one or the other) and PDF (vector, for print).
save_fig <- function(stem, obj, width, height, res = 600) {
  one <- function(open_dev) { open_dev(); on.exit(grDevices::dev.off()); print(obj) }
  one(function() grDevices::png(paste0(stem, ".png"), width = width, height = height,
                                units = "in", res = res))
  one(function() grDevices::jpeg(paste0(stem, ".jpg"), width = width, height = height,
                                 units = "in", res = res, quality = 95))
  pdf_dev <- if (capabilities("cairo")) grDevices::cairo_pdf else grDevices::pdf
  one(function() pdf_dev(paste0(stem, ".pdf"), width = width, height = height))
}
